\begin{abstract}
Intelligent transportation systems in resource-constrained cities need
vehicle detectors that are accurate on overhead imagery and light enough for
modest hardware. This work fine-tunes YOLO11n-OBB, an oriented object detection
model, for nine vehicle categories in Peruvian aerial (nadir) traffic imagery.
We build clip-level train, validation, and test partitions to prevent adjacent
video frames from appearing in different splits. A data-size study trains on
subsets of 1,000, 1,500, 2,000, 2,500, and 3,000 images with the same 50-epoch
protocol and an effective input size of 544 pixels; each subset has its own test
partition. Test mAP@50 rises from 66.2\% with 1,000 images to 91.4\% with 2,500
and then falls to 88.5\% with 3,000, while validation mAP@50 is highest at 3,000.
Because the partitions differ and a single seed is used, differences of a few
points are not conclusive. On the 2,500-image subset, a second
configuration trained for 70 epochs at 640 pixels reaches 93.9\% mAP@50 and
81.7\% mAP@50--95 with 2.66 million parameters; it changes resolution and
epochs together. For edge AI deployment, a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (8~GB RAM)
running NCNN processes the 544-pixel model at 3.33 FPS (300.5 ms mean latency;
305.3 ms P95) and the 640-pixel model at 2.50 FPS (399.9 ms; 405.9 ms P95) over
300 images. The throttling flag was clear at the start and end of each run, but
the temperature was still rising. A compact oriented detector can thus reach
strong accuracy from a modest domain-specific dataset, while the measured
throughput suits low-rate monitoring rather than real-time video.

\keywords{Oriented object detection \and Vehicle detection \and YOLO11n-OBB
\and Edge AI \and Intelligent transportation systems.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Traffic monitoring supports vehicle counting, incident analysis, and urban
planning, and intelligent transportation systems increasingly depend on
automatic vehicle detection. Yet the deployment conditions of many cities make
it impractical to assume a dedicated GPU at every camera, so a useful detector
must balance accuracy with a small computational footprint. This is
particularly relevant for Peruvian road scenes, which contain cars, buses,
microbuses, mototaxis, motorcycles, and trucks, many of them small in the
frame: the shorter side of a typical vehicle spans about 35 pixels in a
$1920\times1080$ image.

Conventional axis-aligned boxes can contain considerable background when a
vehicle is oriented diagonally, and in dense traffic they can overlap heavily
with neighboring vehicles. Oriented bounding boxes (OBBs) encode the extent and
orientation of an object explicitly, an advantage already exploited in aerial
image benchmarks such as DOTA~\cite{xia2018dota,ding2022dota}. The overhead
views studied here share this geometry. We note, however, that most vehicles in
our data are nearly aligned with the image axes (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}), so
the benefit of OBB over horizontal boxes is not tested in this paper.

This paper investigates the fine-tuning of YOLO11n-OBB, a compact member of the
Ultralytics YOLO family that supports OBB prediction~\cite{ultralytics_yolo11,ultralytics_obb}.
We examine how much domain-specific data is needed for useful performance and
benchmark deployment on ARM hardware. The paper makes the following
contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item A reproducible conversion and clip-level splitting protocol for nine
vehicle categories from Peruvian aerial traffic videos.
\item A data-size study from 1,000 to 3,000 images under a fixed 50-epoch
training protocol, with its limitations stated explicitly.
\item A higher-resolution 2,500-image configuration that attains 93.9\%
mAP@50 and 81.7\% mAP@50--95 with 2.66 million parameters.
\item An on-device NCNN benchmark on a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (8~GB RAM) that
quantifies the latency, throughput, CPU use, and thermal behavior of three
trained configurations.
\end{itemize}

Code, split definitions, and checkpoints are available at
\url{https://github.com/jorghee/swole-yolo}.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews related
work. Section~3 describes the dataset, the OBB conversion, the model, and the
training protocol. Section~4 reports detection results, and Section~5 the
Raspberry Pi benchmark. Section~6 discusses limitations and Section~7
concludes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Vehicle Detection and Oriented Bounding Boxes}

One-stage detectors popularized the use of direct, real-time object prediction
from images~\cite{redmon2016yolo}. Vehicle benchmarks such as UA-DETRAC and
BDD100K have made it possible to evaluate detection and tracking in traffic
video, but they do not provide the class composition and OBB annotations used
in this work~\cite{wen2015uadetrac,yu2020bdd100k}. Drone imagery benchmarks,
including VisDrone, further expose the challenges caused by small, crowded, and
overhead-view objects~\cite{zhu2018visdrone}. In aerial imagery,
arbitrary-oriented quadrilaterals are essential because objects exhibit strong
variation in rotation and scale; DOTA provides a widely used benchmark for this
setting~\cite{xia2018dota}.

The current study transfers this representation to overhead traffic imagery,
which is closer to DOTA than to the fixed roadside cameras of UA-DETRAC and
BDD100K. OBBs are not assumed to solve every source of error---occlusion, small
objects, and class imbalance remain difficult---but they provide a closer
geometric description of rotated vehicles than horizontal boxes. RoI
Transformer, R3Det, and Oriented R-CNN exemplify approaches that explicitly
model oriented regions or progressively refine rotated
detections~\cite{ding2019roitransformer,yang2021r3det,xie2021orientedrcnn}.
YOLO11 supports OBB training and inference in the Ultralytics implementation
used here~\cite{ultralytics_obb}.

\subsection{Efficient Inference for Edge Devices}

Embedded deployment requires that model conversion and runtime behavior be
reported separately from training accuracy. NCNN is an open-source inference
framework written in C++ for mobile and embedded devices. It has no third-party
dependencies, includes ARM-specific optimizations, and provides a conversion
path from PyTorch or ONNX models~\cite{ncnn}. We selected it because the
Ultralytics exporter supports it directly and it targets ARM CPUs such as the
Raspberry Pi's; we did not compare it with other runtimes. We export the
selected checkpoints to NCNN and measure their throughput directly on the target
Raspberry Pi. This distinction is important because a desktop CPU measurement,
even with a restricted number of threads, cannot characterize an ARM platform.
Recent Raspberry Pi 4B measurements with YOLOv7-tiny similarly show that the
target runtime and hardware configuration determine the attainable throughput
\cite{santos2024edge}.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Method}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Dataset and OBB Conversion}

The data were released through the MTC SMART Challenge: IA para la Movilidad del
Per\'u on Kaggle~\cite{mtcsmartchallenge}. They consist of $1920\times1080$
frames extracted from video clips of Peruvian urban roads, captured from an
overhead (nadir) aerial viewpoint, with annotations for nine categories:
\textit{car}, \textit{van}, \textit{microbus}, \textit{minibus}, \textit{bus},
\textit{articulated\_bus}, \textit{truck}, \textit{mototaxi}, and
\textit{motorcycle}. Source annotations encode a class identifier, center
coordinates, width, height, and angle in degrees. Each annotation is converted
to the four normalized corner coordinates expected by YOLO-OBB.

For a box centered at $(c_x,c_y)$ with width $w$, height $h$, and orientation
$\theta$, a local corner $(d_x,d_y) \in \{(-w/2,-h/2),(w/2,-h/2),
(w/2,h/2),(-w/2,h/2)\}$ is transformed as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x &= c_x+d_x\cos\theta-d_y\sin\theta,\\
y &= c_y+d_x\sin\theta+d_y\cos\theta.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:obb}
\end{equation}
The resulting coordinates are divided by image width and height and clipped to
$[0,1]$.

Subsets are formed by allocating a quota to each source video and selecting
temporally separated frames within that video. The subsets are nested: each
larger subset contains the smaller ones. The 1,000-image subset contains one
frame from each of 1,000 clips, whereas the larger subsets draw on the same
1,088 clips (on average 1.4 to 2.8 frames per clip), so increasing the subset
size mostly adds frames of scenes that are already represented rather than new
scenes. Images are grouped by clip identifier, and whole clips, rather than
individual frames, are assigned to train, validation, or test. This prevents
near-duplicate adjacent frames from leaking across partitions. The random seed
for a subset of size $n$ is $42+n$, so each subset has its own partition.
Table~\ref{tab:splits} reports the resulting partitions.

Three further properties of the annotations matter for interpretation. First,
vehicles are small: in the 2,500-image subset the median shorter box side is
35 pixels, which becomes about 10 pixels after resizing to a 544-pixel input;
for motorcycles it is 12.8 pixels (about 3.6 pixels at 544). Second, most boxes
are nearly axis-aligned: 60.7\% have an angle of exactly $0^\circ$ and 81.1\%
lie within $2^\circ$ of an image axis, while only 6.9\% deviate by more than
$10^\circ$. Third, in the examples we inspected, parked vehicles outside the
carriageway are often not annotated (for instance the upper right of
Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative}), so the detector learns this annotation convention.
Also, 163 of the 2,500 images (6.5\%) contain no annotated vehicle.

\subsection{Model and Training Protocol}

YOLO11n-OBB is the smallest (nano) variant of YOLO11 with an oriented-box head.
Its backbone and neck consist of 23 layers built from convolution, C3k2,
SPPF, and C2PSA attention blocks, and a single OBB head predicts, at strides 8,
16, and 32 (8,400 candidate locations for a $640\times640$ input), box-side
distributions, class scores, and a rotation angle. The model has 2,655,478
parameters after batch-normalization fusion (2,663,262 before). Training
minimizes the Ultralytics OBB loss, which combines box, classification,
distribution-focal, and angle terms; we did not modify it.

All experiments initialize from \texttt{yolo11n-obb.pt}, which is pretrained on
DOTA. The classification layer is re-initialized for nine classes and all
layers are trained. The fixed scaling protocol uses a batch size of 32,
50 epochs, and a requested image size of 540 pixels. The training runtime aligns
this requested size to the 544-pixel stride-compatible effective input size;
we use the latter when naming the experimental configuration. The model with
2,500 images is also trained for 70 epochs at 640 pixels. In both cases,
patience is 20 epochs and the seed is deterministic. Other settings are
Ultralytics defaults: mosaic augmentation (disabled for the last 10 epochs),
scale 0.5, translation 0.1, horizontal flip 0.5, color jitter, no random
rotation, and automatic optimizer selection.

The checkpoint selected by validation is evaluated once on the held-out test
split. We report precision, recall, mAP at IoU 0.50 (mAP@50), and mAP averaged
over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 (mAP@50--95), following the common COCO
evaluation convention~\cite{lin2014coco}; for oriented boxes, Ultralytics
computes IoU with a probabilistic approximation. Device-side performance is
measured separately with the NCNN export on Raspberry Pi. We do not report
desktop-CPU timings: for models of identical architecture and input size they
carry no information beyond measurement noise.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Data Size Under a Fixed Training Protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:scaling} presents the data-size results. Test mAP@50 increases
from 66.2\% with 1,000 images to 91.4\% with 2,500 images (78.5\% mAP@50--95),
a gain of 25.2 and 23.6 percentage points, respectively, but the trend is not
monotonic: mAP@50 is slightly lower at 2,000 than at 1,500 images (0.813 vs.
0.817) and clearly lower at 3,000 images (0.885) than at 2,500. Because each
subset is evaluated on a clip-level test split of its own, rows of
Table~\ref{tab:scaling} mix the effect of training-set size with differences in
the test sample, so the 3,000-image result cannot be read as a degradation
caused by additional data. Two observations support this reading. First, the
validation mAP@50 of the selected checkpoints, measured on yet another sample of
each subset, ranks the sizes differently: it is highest at 3,000 images (0.895)
and lower at 2,500 (0.860). Validation was used to select the checkpoint, so
these values are mildly optimistic. Second, the 2,500-image checkpoint reaches
0.860 on its validation split and 0.914 on its test split, both with 376 images.
Differences of a few points between partitions thus lie within sampling
variation, and the trend should be read as indicative rather than conclusive.

\section{Raspberry Pi Benchmark}

The three selected checkpoints were exported to NCNN with the Ultralytics
exporter (conversion through PNNX to FP32 \texttt{.param} and \texttt{.bin}
files, with the input size fixed to the training size) and executed through its
NCNN backend on a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B with 8~GB of RAM, running 64-bit Linux
on the \texttt{aarch64} architecture (kernel 6.18.34+rpt-rpi-v8; glibc 2.41).
The available logs do not retain the exact NCNN version or the CPU frequency
governor in effect during the run; we report this remaining limitation
explicitly rather than infer it from the platform. Per-core CPU utilization
(76--95\%) shows that all four cores were used.
Each benchmark uses batch size one, five warm-up images, and a standardized bank
of 300 subsequent images. Reported latency is end-to-end per image and includes
image reading, preprocessing, network execution, and non-maximum suppression.
It does not measure mAP on the device; the detection metrics in
Tables~\ref{tab:scaling} and~\ref{tab:final} are the offline evaluation results
of the source PyTorch checkpoints.

Table~\ref{tab:edge} reports the on-device measurements. At 544 pixels, the
1,000-image and 2,500-image models have nearly identical throughput because
they share the same architecture and input shape. The 2,500-image model is
slightly faster (3.33 FPS) than the 1,000-image model (3.31 FPS), a difference
of 1.96 ms that is not meaningful evidence of a training-set-size effect on
inference speed. Increasing the input size from 544 to 640 pixels increases
mean latency by 99.4 ms (33.1\%) and reduces throughput from 3.33 to 2.50 FPS.

The latency breakdown reported by Ultralytics shows that network execution
accounts for the largest share: 253.6--254.9 ms at 544 pixels and 353.4 ms at
640 pixels, a ratio of 1.39 that closely matches the ratio of pixel counts
($(640/544)^2=1.38$). Preprocessing (9.3--9.5 ms) and non-maximum suppression
(4.2--4.9 ms) are small. The remaining 32--35 ms of the end-to-end latency is
not logged separately; we attribute it to reading and decoding the
$1920\times1080$ image file.

At the recorded start and end checks, the Raspberry Pi throttling flag was
\texttt{0x0} for every run. However, the temperature increased by
17.0--20.5$^\circ$C over the 90--120 s windows, so these short measurements do
not establish thermal steady state or long-duration behavior. Mean CPU
utilization was 81.7--84.1\%. Future benchmarks should record the NCNN thread
count and run long enough to characterize sustained temperature, throttling,
and energy consumption.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The results suggest that a few thousand domain-specific images are enough to
reach strong held-out accuracy for the present domain. Because the
3,000-image subset scores lower than the 2,500-image one on test but higher on
validation, we cannot say whether more data helped beyond 2,500 images. The
data-size
experiment does not estimate uncertainty across random partitions: each data
size has a different clip-level test set and a single training run, and the
larger subsets add frames from the same clips rather than new scenes. A
stronger follow-up study would use a common fixed test set, vary the number of
clips and the number of frames per clip separately, and repeat training over
multiple seeds, reporting confidence intervals.

The study also lacks comparisons that would establish the contribution of its
components: no other detector architecture or model size, no horizontal-box
baseline (important because 81\% of the boxes are nearly axis-aligned), no
from-scratch baseline, and no second hardware platform or inference runtime.
Resolution and training length are changed together in the 640-pixel
configuration. Finally, the labeled data follow an annotation convention that
omits some parked vehicles, so reported accuracy refers to that convention.

The edge benchmark clarifies a practical trade-off. The 640-pixel model offers
the best offline detection quality, but its 2.50 FPS on Raspberry Pi is 24.9\%
lower than the 544-pixel, 2,500-image configuration. Neither configuration
supports a claim of real-time video-rate processing on the tested setup. The
544-pixel model can nevertheless support low-rate sampling, periodic counts, or
event-driven inference where a new decision every 300 ms is acceptable. The
absence of a throttling flag at the recorded endpoints is encouraging, but the
90--120 s test duration is too short to establish sustained thermal performance.

Several limits should guide interpretation. The CPU frequency policy and
NCNN version were not preserved in the benchmark logs, which still leaves a
gap for exact replication even though the board itself (Raspberry Pi 4 Model
B, 8~GB RAM) is now identified. The versions of the training environment and
the training logs were also not retained; re-evaluating the released
checkpoints with Ultralytics 8.4.211 reproduces the reported test metrics to
within 0.001. The device-side test did not recompute mAP, so the paper does not
claim numerical equivalence between the PyTorch and NCNN outputs. The breadth of
geographic coverage, nighttime scenes, adverse weather, and camera viewpoints
must be documented before making broader generalization claims. Future work can
record the missing hardware metadata, use a labeled on-device equivalence test,
assess power over multi-hour runs, and combine detection with multi-object
tracking for vehicle counting and trajectory analysis.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a clip-level evaluation of YOLO11n-OBB for oriented vehicle
detection in Peruvian aerial traffic imagery and an on-device NCNN benchmark on
Raspberry Pi. Under a fixed 50-epoch protocol, test mAP@50 rose from 66.2\% with
1,000 images to 91.4\% with 2,500 and fell to 88.5\% with 3,000, with each
subset evaluated on its own test partition and a single run, so the trend is
indicative rather than conclusive. A 70-epoch, 640-pixel configuration reached
93.9\% mAP@50 and 81.7\% mAP@50--95 with 2.66 million parameters. On the
Raspberry Pi, the 2,500-image 544-pixel model achieved 3.33 FPS, whereas the
more accurate 640-pixel model achieved 2.50 FPS. The results demonstrate
low-rate edge inference in short runs; a fixed common test set, multiple seeds,
baselines, additional runtimes and hardware, and longer thermal tests are the
necessary next steps before drawing stronger conclusions or deploying for
real-time use.
